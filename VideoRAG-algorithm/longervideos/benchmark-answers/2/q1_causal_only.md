Question: Why did the triple product in fusion research stop accelerating in the late 1990s/early 2000s?

### Overview of Fusion Research Plateau

Fusion research plateaued during the late 1990s and early 2000s, particularly concerning the triple product (a measure used to gauge fusion performance) in magnetic confinement devices. This period saw a significant slowdown in advancements compared to earlier decades when progress was rapid and surpassing Moore's Law.

### Key Factors Contributing to the Plateau

1. **Financial Constraints**: One of the main reasons cited is the financial challenge required for building larger experimental devices necessary to achieve breakthroughs. Estimates range from $30 billion to $60 billion, emphasizing that such ventures demand substantial investment over several decades.

2. **Technical Challenges**: Building bigger and more complex devices became increasingly challenging due to engineering risks and technological limitations at the time. For instance, there were constraints in constructing large-scale magnetic components needed for fusion experiments, as discussed by a panelist wearing a dark suit.

3. **Complexity of Experiments**: Fusion technology involves not only making massive devices but also ensuring they are reliable, maintainable, and capable of sustained operation — aspects that add to the complexity beyond sheer physical size. This was highlighted during discussions about magnetic mirror projects which faced difficulties in scaling up due to these complexities.

### Contextual Insights from Panel Discussions

During a panel discussion at Columbia Engineering, speakers emphasized the need for human cooperation and ingenuity to overcome these hurdles. They underscored how significant investments are required not just in building infrastructure but also in fostering collaborations across various sectors — academia, industry, government, and beyond.

Panelists discussed historical challenges faced during this period, including technical setbacks like the termination of magnetic mirror programs due to their ambitious nature being ahead of existing capabilities. These discussions highlighted the importance of reevaluating strategies and focusing on more capital-efficient approaches.

### Moving Forward

The panel also addressed current trends suggesting that with advancements in technology (e.g., high-temperature superconductors) and shifting investment landscapes, there is renewed optimism about overcoming previous limitations. There are efforts to accelerate timelines for key aspects while maintaining ambitious goals.

In conclusion, the late 1990s/early 2000s saw a plateau due primarily to financial, technical, and engineering challenges that required significant resources beyond what was initially anticipated. However, ongoing research and evolving strategies offer hope for overcoming these obstacles in future fusion endeavors.
