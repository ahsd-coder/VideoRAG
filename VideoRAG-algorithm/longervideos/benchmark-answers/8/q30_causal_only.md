### Studying Injury Tolerance in Spines with Pre-Existing Degenerative Conditions

**Introduction**
Exponent addresses the challenge of studying injury tolerance in spines with pre-existing degenerative conditions by conducting comprehensive biomechanical analyses. This involves evaluating the mechanical properties and injury tolerance of spines with specific degenerative conditions like Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS) and Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH).

**Key Methods and Findings**

1. **Experimental Design**
   - **Paired Specimens**: Exponent obtained paired specimens, one with DISH (Dynamic Impact Spinal Hypertrophy) and one without, to assess differences in mechanical properties and injury tolerance.
   - **Factors Assessed**: The study evaluated stiffness, flexion-extension range of motion, and extension loading tolerance in these specimens.
   
2. **Degenerative Conditions**
   - **Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS)**: An inflammatory disease causing fusion of spinal discs, facet joints, and ligaments.
   - **Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH)**: Characterized by excess bone formation leading to stiffening of the spine due to fused vertebrae.
   
3. **Biomechanical Laboratory Studies**
   - **Testing Environment**: The specimens were tested in a biomechanics laboratory, simulating real-world accident scenarios to evaluate spinal responses.
   - **Controlled Conditions**: Ensuring consistency in testing methods to accurately measure the impact of pre-existing conditions on injury tolerance.
   
4. **Visual Comparisons**
   - **Normal vs. DISH**: Side-by-side comparisons of normal and DISH spines, highlighting differences in disc space preservation and bridging bone formations.
   - **Structural Differences**: Demonstrated through images and 3D models, illustrating the stiffer and less flexible nature of DISH spines compared to normal spines.

**Conclusion**
Exponent’s approach to studying injury tolerance in spines with pre-existing degenerative conditions involves rigorous experimental designs and detailed biomechanical assessments. By obtaining paired specimens and conducting controlled tests, they aim to bridge the gap in understanding how these conditions affect spinal injury tolerance, ultimately contributing to better injury prevention strategies and treatment options.
